Policy brief

Closing the literacy gap
in AUKUS export controls.

Prepared by Ari U · Canberra, August 2026 · Research brief, not legal advice

The opportunity

On 1 September 2024, Australia’s Defence Trade Controls Amendment Act 2024 commenced alongside a US ITAR §126.7 exemption, establishing a licence-free environment for most defence trade among Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States.12 Australia’s Deputy Prime Minister described the result as licence-free trade for “over 70 per cent” of ITAR-subject exports and “over 80 per cent” of EAR-subject trade from the US to Australia.3 The US Commerce Department places the figure at “about 80% of the value” of existing commercial defence trade.4 A final rule published on 30 December 2025 implemented the August 2024 interim final rule “with changes,” adding ITAR §126.7(c) for transfers supporting deployed AUKUS forces; more than 700 Australian and UK persons are now enrolled as Authorised Users.5

The gap that remains

The exemption is not universal. Items on the Excluded Technologies List (Supplement No. 2 to 22 CFR Part 126) remain subject to standard licensing — from MTCR-annotated missiles and cluster munitions to F-22 articles, anti-tamper features, manufacturing know-how and classified countermeasures.6 The United States Studies Centre estimates the ETL captures the ~30% of intra-AUKUS trade not covered by the exemption, “deliberately” spanning technologies central to both Pillar I and Pillar II.7

The harder problem is human, not legal. Greenwalt and Corben argue that “small and medium-sized companies who lack the in-house expertise or financial capital to manage these compliance requirements are disincentivised from working with the US defence ecosystem,” and that unaddressed these costs “will deter innovative start-ups from contributing potentially game-changing technologies to AUKUS programs.”8 Piper Alderman similarly frames ITAR as “the biggest impediment to realising a truly integrated defence and technology enterprise,” with some authorisations taking “upwards of six months.”9

Why this matters for Australia

The affected tier is not marginal. Small and mid-sized suppliers are precisely the feeders into Australia’s prime contractors and the wider defence supply chain, and the source of much of the country’s niche defence innovation.8 Every supplier who mis-reads the ETL either over-restricts (lost revenue, slowed programmes) or under-restricts (compliance breach). Australia’s own licence-free environment imposes five cumulative criteria — eligible end-user, AUKUS location, items off the Excluded Goods & Technologies List, registered Authorised User status, and pre-notification via the MADE portal — each of which a non-expert can misapply.10 The Defence Trade Controls Amendment Act 2024 also created three new offences (ss. 10A–10C) for unauthorised supply to foreign persons and sensitive-list transfers.1

What a navigator does

A plain-English triage tool cannot replace classification or legal review — and should not pretend to. What it can do is convert a vague question (“can I ship this?”) into a structured one: which regime, which destination, which recipient, which ETL category, which evidence pack. By returning a decision trail with cited rules rather than an opaque verdict, it makes the supplier’s next step explicit — and makes the boundary between the tool’s judgement and professional review legible. This is the “export-control literacy” lift the USSC identifies as missing, delivered at the point of friction rather than in a 60-page PDF.

Recommendation

Defence industry associations, prime contractors and government outreach programs should pilot cited triage tools as a complement to (not substitute for) formal classification and the MADE portal. Such tools should be explicitly scoped as research aids, surface the ETL and the five Australian licence-free criteria — eligible end-user, AUKUS location, non-excluded goods, Authorised User registration, and MADE pre-notification — at the point of decision, and route every result to professional review where classification is unclear or sensitive categories are touched. Done well, this reduces both failure modes — over- and under-restriction — and improves the absorptive capacity on which AUKUS Pillar II ultimately depends.1112
