Lens one

Critical infrastructure, under fire and online

Water systems are now targets in three ways: physical strikes in wartime, cyber intrusion in peacetime, and quiet competition for supply from energy and AI infrastructure.

The Gulf: “saltwater kingdoms” in a war zone

Desalination met 60% of Gulf Cooperation Council water consumption in 2023 and 76% of its municipal drinking-grade supply. AP reported that a 2010 CIA analysis warned attacks on these plants could trigger national crises in several Gulf states, and that more than 90% of the Gulf's desalinated water comes from just 56 plants.

Since the US–Israel war with Iran began on 28 February 2026, several desalination facilities have been reported damaged. The Arab Center Washington DC notes that damage has so far been limited, with no significant disruption to supply reported. As of early November the war was in its ninth month, with diplomacy and strikes running in parallel.

International humanitarian law, including the Geneva Conventions, prohibits attacking civilian infrastructure indispensable to the survival of the population, which includes drinking-water facilities.

Cyber: the quiet front line

In February 2024, CISA, the NSA and the FBI assessed that China-backed actors known as Volt Typhoon were pre-positioning on the networks of critical infrastructure, including water and wastewater systems, for possible disruption during a crisis or conflict. Australia's Cyber Security Centre co-sealed the advisory and assessed that Australian infrastructure could be vulnerable to similar activity.

In July 2026, more than 100 internet-exposed systems in the US water sector were hit by cyberattacks, prompting CISA guidance on finding exposed industrial controllers.

Criminology angle

Attacks on water systems sit across the lines between crime, terrorism and statecraft. The same exposed controller can be reached by a criminal group, a hacktivist or a state, so attribution, and with it the choice of legal response, is often the hardest part.

This is why water-sector cyber resilience belongs in both the conference's investment conversation and national security policy.

The water–energy–AI nexus, and a measurement problem

UN University estimates data centres will use 945 terawatt-hours of electricity by 2030, with a water footprint equal to the basic annual domestic needs of all 1.3 billion people in Sub-Saharan Africa. Rystad Energy, counting only water consumed directly for cooling, puts 2030 at about 644 billion litres in its central case.

Both can be right, because they measure different things. One counts water evaporated in the building; the other adds the water used to generate the building's power. Google's widely quoted 0.26 mL for a median Gemini text prompt counts cooling water only, not water used to generate electricity.

Lens two

Trigger, weapon, casualty

The Pacific Institute's Water Conflict Chronology, the largest open-source database of water-related violence, sorts each event by the role water plays: a trigger of violence, a weapon, or a casualty. It now holds more than 2,750 incidents.

Where cooperation is missing

Shared waters carry 60% of global freshwater flows and directly sustain more than 40% of the world's population. Only around 10 agreements govern specific transboundary aquifers worldwide.

As of August 2026 the UN Water Convention had 59 parties and the UN Watercourses Convention 42; twelve countries have acceded since 2023.

Cooperation pays

A flood early-warning system on the Ratu River between Nepal and India reportedly saved 169 lives in Nepal during the 2017 floods.

Rising violence and thin legal cover point the same way: the cheapest security investment on the table in Abu Dhabi may be treaty accessions and joint basin bodies, not hardware.

From Antalya to Abu Dhabi: Australia's water month

Three things for Australian readers

Pacific credibility. Australia's COP31 role was built on Pacific priorities. Pacific water and sanitation gaps are a direct test of that partnership. DFAT and the Australian Water Partnership, a DFAT-funded program implemented by eWater Group, have supported the Pacific Water and Wastewater Association for more than a decade.

The AI water question at home. Data centres use under 1% of Greater Sydney's water today, about 3.5 billion litres a year. On 23 March 2026 the federal government put sustainable water use into its data centre investment expectations for the first time.

Inclusion. In 2024, Australia, Canada and New Zealand said they were disappointed the UN did not uphold best practice on civil society participation in the conference.

Why this lens is rare

Most Abu Dhabi coverage will come from the Gulf, Europe and New York. An Indo-Pacific reading connects three files that Canberra usually runs separately: climate diplomacy, Pacific development and critical-infrastructure security.

Watch for whether Australia carries COP31 Pacific language into Abu Dhabi, and whether it announces water commitments for the Pacific.
