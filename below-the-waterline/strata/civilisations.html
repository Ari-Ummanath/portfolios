When the rains failed

For each case, the climate signal comes from a natural archive (ocean dust, river sediments, pollen, lake gypsum or tree rings) and the human story from archaeology and texts. Read them as evidence of pressure, not proof of a single cause.

What the six cases share

Every society here had built something remarkable on reliable water: grain lands in northern Mesopotamia, flood-fed farming on the Indus, Angkor's network of canals and reservoirs. Each then met a drought lasting decades to centuries, recorded independently in natural archives.

None of the studies claims water alone ended a civilisation. People also moved and adapted: Mesa Verde's farmers headed for places where maize could still grow, and the Indus population shifted toward wetter regions.

The same infrastructure that made these societies thrive also made them dependent on water arriving on schedule. That is the link to the present: modern cities, irrigation and AI data centres are also built on assumptions about water that a changing climate can break.
