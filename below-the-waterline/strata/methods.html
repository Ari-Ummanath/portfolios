Method & sources

How this was built, and where it stops

This page sets out what counts as evidence here, how the timeline works, and what the project cannot tell you.

Five rules

1. Peer review first. Ancient cases rest on peer-reviewed paleoclimate studies. Modern cases use peer-reviewed research, UN and government assessments, and dated reporting for recent events.

2. No invented numbers. The simulator shows only figures stated in a source. It does not interpolate between years or model new projections. Where a study gives a range, the range is shown.

3. Derived values are marked. Two bars in the water-conflict chart are calculated from stated percentage changes; they are hatched and labelled approximate.

4. Causation is not assumed. Each case carries a “debate” line. A drought coinciding with a collapse is evidence of pressure, not proof of cause.

5. Analysis is labelled. Interpretive lines are marked “Analysis” so readers can separate the author's judgement from the evidence.

Why the timeline stretches

The slider runs from 3000 BCE to 2100 on a piecewise scale: 3000 BCE to 1 CE fills about a third of its length, 1 CE to 1900 another third, and 1900 to 2100 the final third. On a straight scale the last two centuries would be a sliver. Real sediment cores behave similarly, because older layers are compressed by the weight above them.

Dates in ancient cases are approximate. The Akkadian aridity onset, for example, is dated to 4025 ± 125 calendar years before present, so the marker's start year is a central estimate.

Limitations

The cases are a curated set of well-studied examples, not a census of every drought. Regions with less research, including much of Africa and Australia before colonisation, are under-represented; an empty map means missing research, not missing drought.

Proxies are local or regional; a marker is placed at the study site, not across the full area affected. The 2023 water-stress shading uses the Aqueduct baseline for every later year, because country-level projections are not shown.

Projection figures come from different models with different definitions (water stress, water scarcity, water footprint) and should not be compared directly.

About

Strata is an independent evidence project by Ari, an undergraduate in International Security Studies and Arts (Criminology) at the Australian National University. It is part of Below the Waterline, a live brief on the 2026 UN Water Conference in Abu Dhabi.

Every figure was checked against the source in its note. Not affiliated with the United Nations or any organisation cited.
