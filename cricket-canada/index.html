Cricket Canada: An Intelligence-Analyst Read on a Live ICC Case

Last updated 18 September 2026 · Canberra, Australia

Executive summary

On 1 June 2026 the ICC suspended Cricket Canada's full membership over "serious breaches of its membership obligations" (Global News). That is the endpoint so far of a case that started as one suspicious over in a T20 World Cup match and grew into an organised-crime story.

A CBC Fifth Estate documentary, Corruption, Crime and Cricket, aired 17 April 2026 alleging the Bishnoi network — a transnational syndicate Canada designated a terrorist entity on 30 September 2025 — had reached into the selection of national captain Dilpreet Bajwa (CBC; Tribune India). Bajwa's fifth over against New Zealand in Chennai on 17 February 2026 — a no-ball, a wide, 15 runs conceded — is what the ICC Anti-Corruption Unit (ACU) is formally investigating (Al Jazeera).

Five months on, Cricket Canada is suspended, its ICC funding — 63% of its revenue — is frozen and gated through an ICC-controlled mechanism, and a Normalisation Committee holds confidential reinstatement conditions (Yahoo Sports; Global News; Cricket Canada). No individual has been charged.

This brief reads the case the way an intelligence analyst would: as a worked example of how betting-anomaly detection feeds ACU casework, and as the basis for an original argument — the diaspora networks the Bishnoi gang exploited to reach Cricket Canada are the same networks that, engaged deliberately, could function as an early-warning and reporting asset for the ACU rather than only a vulnerability.

Three things depart from the brief's original framing. First, the trigger was not a betting-monitoring alert but investigative journalism — the ACU says it acted on the CBC broadcast, not the reverse. Second, the allegation is not narrowly about a fixed over; it is about a designated terrorist organisation allegedly shaping who captains a national team. Third, the consequence has already moved from investigation to sanction: Cricket Canada is suspended and defunded five months before any individual has been charged with anything.

Part II — How anomaly detection feeds ACU casework

The ICC Anti-Corruption Unit runs a four-part model — prevention, disruption, investigation, prosecution — out of a central hub in Dubai. Andrew Ephgrave (former British police officer) is interim general manager; Sumathi Dharmawardena PC, formerly of Sri Lanka's Attorney General's Department and with Interpol and UNODC experience, chairs it. The unit is staffed by former law-enforcement professionals and places Anti-Corruption Managers in India, South Africa, New Zealand, Pakistan and England, coordinating with 12 Full Member domestic-board ACUs (including the BCCI, ECB and Cricket Australia) and an expanding set of Associate Member ACUs (ICC).

In the model the original brief assumes, a case starts with a betting-pattern alert: licensed bookmakers and monitoring agencies flag abnormal wagering on a specific market — the total runs in an over, a no-ball, a top bowler — and the ACU opens a file, then works backward to the player, the passer of information, and the syndicate behind it. "Trusted insiders" (current or former players) and encrypted messaging apps are the ACU's own stated concern in that pathway (ICC).

The Canada case inverts that sequence, and that inversion is the analytically interesting part. Ephgrave's own statement was that the ACU is "aware of the programme broadcast by CBC" — language that credits investigative journalism, not a betting-market trigger, with surfacing the case (Al Jazeera). Two explanations are both plausible from the open-source record and are not mutually exclusive: either the anomaly in Bajwa's fifth over was too small or too associate-market-thin to trip a monitoring threshold on its own, or it did generate an alert that simply hadn't produced a public case before CBC's reporting forced the pace. Associate nations play far fewer internationally broadcast, heavily bookmaked matches than Full Members, which likely means thinner liquidity and weaker betting-market signal in exactly the fixtures where governance is often also weakest — a structural blind spot the pure anomaly-detection model does not obviously solve on its own.

That gap is what Part III turns to.

Part III — The diaspora angle: an early-warning asset, not only a vulnerability

The same diaspora channels the Bishnoi network is alleged to have used against Cricket Canada are ones it uses more broadly. Canadian intelligence reporting describes the network exploiting the student-visa stream to build a base inside Canada, and the gang itself has claimed over 1,000 "foot soldiers" in the country in a letter to police (Tribune India; Tribune India). Its Canadian operations run on extortion and intimidation aimed squarely at the Punjabi diaspora — including a 2025 shooting at the Ontario home of Punjabi singer Channi Nattan, claimed by the gang (Tribune India), and the assassination of Sidhu Moosewala that led directly to Canada's terrorist designation of the group on 30 September 2025 (Tribune India). A national cricket federation drawing its administrators, coaches and players overwhelmingly from that same Punjabi-Canadian community was never an unlikely target — it was a predictable one, sitting inside a diaspora ecosystem organised crime was already working.

The ACU's centralised, Dubai-based, betting-alert-driven model has no natural sensor inside that ecosystem. What actually surfaced this case was community-adjacent: a CBC investigative team working sources close to Cricket Canada, and a recorded call from a former coach describing internal pressure. That information existed inside the community long before it reached a regulator — it travelled through journalism because there was no formal channel for it to travel through integrity structures instead.

The original argument this case supports: structured, protected engagement between ACU/member-board integrity units and diaspora cricket community networks — club committees, umpiring and coaching associations, diaspora sports media, community elders — could function as a genuine early-warning and reporting asset, not merely a governance risk to be managed from a distance. These are the people positioned to notice a captaincy decision that doesn't track merit, a coach describing pressure, a player suddenly flush with money, well before any of it shows up as a betting-market anomaly on a lightly-traded Associate fixture.

This is not a naive proposal. It runs directly into the same infiltration risk it is meant to counter: Cricket Canada's own alleged compromise involved its board, so a diaspora-reporting channel cannot simply feed the federation's own leadership — exactly the actor the Chohan allegation implicates (CBC). It would need to sit with the ACU or an independent domestic-board integrity unit directly, carry real anonymity and anti-retaliation protection, and be built with, not merely aimed at, diaspora community organisations — or it simply becomes one more channel for a compromised board, or an infiltrating network, to capture. Nobody in the current public-reporting or policy literature on ACU casework appears to be making this argument for cricket specifically; it is the genuinely unfilled niche the brief identified, and the Canada case is the concrete evidence for why it matters now rather than in the abstract.
