How a firebreak works

A firebreak stops a fire by clearing the ground it needs to spread. Checking DNA orders can do the same for a biological threat.

AI is making dangerous orders harder to spot, and Australia doesn't yet require suppliers to check them. Firebreak proposes an AI-assisted screening gateway that Australia can switch on with laws it already has.

Firebreak

Scientists order custom DNA much like a print job: they send a sequence and a supplier makes it. Suppliers check those orders so nobody can buy the building blocks of a biological weapon. AI is making those checks easier to fool, and Australia does not require them at all.

This project sets out one fix: an AI-assisted screening gateway that Australia can switch on with laws it already has. It shows AI working on both sides, as the threat and as the defence, and costs the plan using the best published benchmark.

The problem

DNA orders are a chokepoint, and AI is loosening it

Researchers order synthetic DNA from commercial suppliers, and those suppliers screen orders to stop dangerous material being made. That makes the order a natural checkpoint. Four facts explain why it matters now.

October 2025, Science

AI can disguise dangerous orders

A Microsoft-led study found AI protein-design tools could redesign known toxins so that many versions might evade the screening DNA companies use.

The good news: the same team built a fix with industry and shared it with DNA synthesis companies worldwide.

April 2025, benchmark study

AI knows the lab work

OpenAI's o3 scored 43.8% on a hard virology troubleshooting test. Expert virologists averaged 22.1%.

As know-how gets easier to find, the physical materials become a more important barrier.

May 2026, Australia

Australia has no screening rule

Australian import permits do not require suppliers to screen DNA orders or check customers. Most countries lack explicit screening rules.

148 experts and 12 organisations asked the Agriculture Minister to close the gap.

November 2026, United States

Allies are moving

The US now requires federally funded labs to buy DNA only from certified suppliers that screen orders of 50 or more nucleotides. A UK study recommends legislation by late 2026.

Orders could shift to countries with weaker rules.

The problem

What “DNA to order” means

Here, “DNA to order” means genetic material built by a machine to a sequence the customer chooses, rather than taken from a living thing. A researcher sends the sequence, and a supplier or a desktop machine in their lab makes it.

“DNA” is used for short. The US policy and the UK study use the wider term “synthetic nucleic acids,” which also covers RNA, and this proposal follows them.

Inside the boundary

  • Orders of 50 nucleotides or more. This is the threshold the US policy uses and the UK study recommends.
  • Orders from overseas suppliers shipped into Australia, which already pass through the import permit system.
  • Orders from suppliers based in Australia.
  • Desktop machines that let a lab print DNA itself, brought in during Phase 3, following the US approach of authorising each print.

Outside the boundary

These are this proposal's choices, to keep the gateway focused.

  • Orders under 50 nucleotides.
  • Living organisms, natural samples and cell lines. They are not made to order, so they are not part of this gateway.
  • The AI design tools themselves. The gateway checks what gets made, not the software that designed it.
  • Approving the research. The gateway checks the order and the customer. It does not decide which experiments go ahead.

The proposal

An AI-assisted screening gateway for Australia

Every synthetic DNA order entering or made in Australia passes one gateway: screened, checked against the customer, reviewed by a person if flagged, and kept current by AI that tests the system before attackers do.

What makes it different: Australia can switch this on without new legislation, using import-permit powers it already has, and use AI on both sides, as the threat and as the defence.

The proposal

How AI defends the gateway

The same technology that creates the problem is the fastest way to keep the defence current.

Red-teaming

Attack your own system first

Red-teaming means attacking your own system on purpose, with permission, to find weak spots before a real attacker does. Defenders use AI design tools to probe screening this way. It is how the 2025 Science study found the gap, and it led to a fix shared with industry.

Smarter screening

Keep tools up to date

The US policy calls for a "tech-enabled screening layer that keeps pace with evolving risks," with screening tools verified by NIST. Free tools such as IBBIS's Common Mechanism and SecureDNA are updated centrally.

Triage

Focus people on the risky few

The US policy describes risk-based screening powered by advanced AI. AI sorts routine orders from unusual ones, so human reviewers spend their time where it matters.

Evaluation

Test new AI tools as they launch

Australia's new AI Safety Institute can test new biological AI tools against the gateway, as the UK is doing with its £100M AI Biosecurity Lab and support from AI companies.
