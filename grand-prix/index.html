01 — The Governance Gap

Nobody's Checked the Math Since 2007

Every economic figure attached to the Australian Grand Prix since 2007 has come from a commissioned consultancy. The one time Victoria's own Auditor-General tested those figures independently, the result contradicted every claim made since — and no one has run that test again.

The proof — this isn't a claim, it's on the public record

In 2006, the Victorian Auditor-General's Office commissioned an outside firm, Applied Economics, to independently test the economic case for the Grand Prix using the same cost-benefit method used to evaluate any other piece of public spending. The finding was published, tabled in Parliament, and has never been repeated since.

"The estimated costs exceed the benefits to Victoria by 5 per cent." — a net social benefit of −$3.8 million, benefit-cost ratio 0.95, for the 2005 Grand Prix.

Why this matters for a $3B, 15-year commitment: the Victorian Government's own audited numbers — not a critic's estimate — show a net cash cost of $102.33M for the Grand Prix in FY2024-25 alone, plus $26.63M for the co-hosted MotoGP, a combined $128.96M in one year. That figure is not contested by anyone. The benefit side of the ledger is contested by almost everyone qualified to assess it.
02 — The Framework

Three Numbers, Not One

Every public dispute over the Grand Prix's value comes from collapsing three different questions into a single headline figure. This framework keeps them apart on purpose.

What "framework" actually means here, and how it's used

A framework isn't one number — it's a repeatable method. Instead of everyone quoting whichever figure a press release hands them, this is a fixed set of steps that produces the same kind of answer no matter who applies it or which year they're looking at. In practice: take the numbers AGPC already publishes (attendance, visitor spend, government cost), run them through three separate calculations instead of one blended one, and compare what comes out to whatever number is in the headline that year. You can see it actually done, step by step, on the 2026 worked example page.

Here's why the separation matters. Imagine a company that only ever publishes its revenue, never its costs. Technically every number it reports is true — and the picture it paints is still deeply misleading. That's roughly what's happening here: government communications report a single "economic benefit" figure and stop, without publishing the cost side of the ledger next to it, and without saying which of three very different questions that figure is actually answering.

Those three questions — and why keeping them separate actually matters — are below.

Question 1How much did visitors actually spend here, that they wouldn't have spent anyway?
Question 2Does that spending ripple outward and create more value elsewhere in the economy — and how much?
Question 3Separate from dollars entirely — what does hosting this event do for how the world sees Melbourne and Australia?
1
Direct Activity

Direct Economic Activity

Verified, near-cash spending by out-of-state visitors whose main reason for travelling was the event — not gross turnover, and not money that would have been spent in Victoria anyway.

  • Bottom-up: (out-of-state attendees) × (verified average spend) × (% primary-purpose)
  • Net of the government's own subsidy, which moves money around rather than creating it
  • Sourced from AGPC's audited annual report and its own visitor-exit survey
2
Multiplier Range

Indirect / Induced Multiplier Range

A "multiplier" is the number economists multiply raw visitor spending by, to try to capture money circulating further through the economy. Say a visitor spends $100 at a restaurant — the waiter paid from that $100 spends some of it too, and so does whoever they pay next. A multiplier of, say, 1.9 claims that original $100 eventually generates $190 in total activity once those ripples are counted. The catch: nobody agrees how big the ripple really is, and the number you pick can nearly double the final figure — which is exactly why "flow-on effects" are the single most disputed part of every event study.

  • Low bound — no ripple counted: just the spending itself, no multiplier. The Bureau of Statistics stopped publishing its own official multipliers in 2001–02, judging them too easy to misuse this way
  • High bound — industry multiplier: the ripple effect implied by industry-commissioned models, roughly 1.85–2.0× the original spending
  • Instead of picking one and hiding the disagreement, the framework shows both ends — the size of that gap is the political argument, made visible instead of buried in a footnote
3
Soft-Power Index

Soft-Power / Diplomatic-Value Index

An illustrative 0–100 composite for the value dollar-based models simply can't price: global visibility, destination brand lift, and relationships between host nations.

  • Weighted sub-scores anchored to real, cited data — not vibes
  • Grounded in published research on soft power and sports mega-events
  • Marked down on purpose where the case is self-reported or genuinely contested

Why should anyone outside Treasury care?

Because "the Grand Prix generates $300M" and "the Grand Prix costs Victoria $100M a year" are both true statements that can appear in the same news cycle — and almost nobody explains how. Once you see the three-way split, every headline about the Grand Prix's value becomes something you can actually evaluate, instead of something you either believe or don't based on who said it.

Scale that same approach up to today's much bigger government subsidy, and 2026 would likely land somewhere between roughly breaking even and a loss too — though nobody can say exactly how much without someone actually running the numbers again. Getting that real, independent study done is this framework's main recommendation.

Okay, but why do these three numbers not match?

Picture three different people asked to value the same concert ticket. One counts every dollar the fan spent that weekend — Uber, hotel, dinner — and calls that the concert's value. A second says: that spending doesn't just stop there, it pays a waiter who then spends it too, so the "real" value is bigger — maybe twice as big, depending how generous your assumptions are. A third points out the promoter still had to pay for the venue, security, and staging, and asks whether the concert actually turned a profit once you subtract that.

  1. The ~$324M figure is the second person's answer: gross visitor spending, run through a multiplier that boosts it further. It's a legitimate way to estimate economic activity — but it's not the same thing as "profit," and the Bureau of Statistics itself stopped publishing the official version of this multiplier in 2001–02 because it was so easy to misuse this way.
  2. The ~$107M figure is closer to the first person's answer: real, traceable visitor spending, without the multiplier boost, and without double-counting money that would have been spent in Victoria regardless.
  3. The −$102.3M figure is the third person's answer, and it's the only one that's actually audited rather than modelled: what it cost the Victorian government, in cash, once ticket and sponsorship revenue is subtracted from what was spent putting the event on.

None of the three numbers is "wrong." They're answers to three different questions, and the entire public argument about the Grand Prix happens because the media, government press releases, and critics each usually only quote one of the three — the one that best fits the point they're making.

Who this actually matters to, and why

The general public

You're the one whose taxes cover the $102M-a-year gap if the "benefit" side is overstated — and whose community sport, hospitals, or transport budget competes with it for the same dollars.

Government & Treasury

A transparent, defensible framework is what protects a $3B, 15-year public commitment from becoming a political liability at the next election or budget estimates hearing.

Investors & industry

Consistent multiplier-model disputes are exactly what erode confidence in "economic activity" claims generally — a credible, repeatable framework rebuilds that trust for future major-event bids.

Media & recruiters

It shows the difference between repeating a headline number and being able to explain where it actually came from.

Live case study · July 2026

When One Country Rents Another Country's Race

Sepang, Malaysia — hosting the "Formula 1 Gulf Air Bahrain Grand Prix," 2–4 October 2026

In July 2026, F1 and the FIA confirmed something unusual: Malaysia's Sepang International Circuit will host a round of the championship on 2–4 October 2026 — but it will still be called the Bahrain Grand Prix. Bahrain's own venue faced hosting challenges for the date; rather than drop the round, F1 arranged for Malaysia to run it physically while Bahrain keeps the name, the branding, and the diplomatic credit.

Malaysia's Deputy Prime Minister was candid about the economics: with Bahrain covering primary hosting costs, Malaysia's own commitment is close to its existing RM16 million annual Sepang maintenance budget — while it banks the "tourism, foreign investment, and global exposure" regardless of whose name is on the race. The Prime Minister framed it explicitly as a soft-power move: "enduring trust between Malaysia and Bahrain," and proof of Malaysia's "capability to host major international events." The former Sepang CEO was blunter — calling it a "litmus test" for whether Malaysia earns a permanent race back, after "astronomical hosting fees" pushed it off the calendar between 2017 and 2026.

This deal is a real-world instance of exactly the split this framework argues for. Bahrain keeps the soft-power asset — the name, the flag, the diplomatic story — without bearing the hosting cost. Malaysia takes on the economic activity and the operational cost, in exchange for a low-risk "audition" for future soft-power value of its own. F1 itself is treating who pays and who gets the prestige as two separate, tradeable things — which is precisely why this framework refuses to collapse them into one number for Melbourne.

The real picture

The index scores well on global broadcast reach and on Formula 1's own revealed preference — a 15-year contract is a strong signal from the promoter's side. It scores modestly on genuine international-guest diversity: roughly 98% of attendees are Australian, and most of the "visitor economy" is interstate, not international. The soft-power case for Melbourne is real but narrower than the "F1 as global nation-branding" framing used for Gulf-state hosts — and, as the Malaysia case above shows, that framing is itself something governments openly trade on, contest, and sometimes rent out to someone else entirely.

05 — The Gameplan

Making It Worth It

Almost every public conversation about the Grand Prix asks a yes/no question: is it worth it? That debate has run, unresolved, for nineteen years — see the governance gap above. This page asks the question almost nobody asks instead. Victoria has already committed to hosting Formula 1 through 2037. Given that, what would it actually take to make that commitment pay off — for local businesses, for government, for the corporation that runs it, and for the public who ultimately carries the cost?

"Impact" and "leverage" are not the same thing — and the difference is the whole point of this page

Sport-management researcher Laurence Chalip drew a distinction that most public debate about events collapses: an event's impact is what happens whether or not anyone plans for it — visitors turn up, they spend money, some of that ripples outward. Its leverage is the separate, deliberate set of strategies a host uses to capture more value from an event that is happening anyway. Think of hosting a big dinner party: the food, the noise, and the cleanup happen regardless. Whether your guests leave with a neighbour's business card, buy something from the stall next door, or you use the goodwill to ask a favour later — that's a choice, not a byproduct.

Every other page on this site measures impact — what the Grand Prix generates or costs, whether planned for or not. This page is different on purpose: it does not re-argue whether Victoria should keep hosting Formula 1 — that decision is already locked in by contract to 2037. It asks what genuine, research-backed leverage would look like if applied to an event Victoria is hosting regardless, and how far that is from what's currently documented.

A gap, not a verdict

An absence of public documentation is not proof that nothing happens — AGPC may run supplier or community programs that simply aren't reported the way Gold Coast 2018's were. The point of this page isn't to accuse; it's to propose closing that same transparency gap this site documents everywhere else, regardless of which way any future independent cost-benefit review eventually lands.

A Plan for Four Stakeholders

Concrete, cited steps — grouped by who they're for, not by what sounds good in a press release.

1
Local Businesses & SMEs

Leverage, Not Luck

  1. A formal SME leverage program, launched a year out — pre-event training, matchmaking with AGPC and F1-linked suppliers, and digital-readiness grants, modelled directly on Gold Coast 2018's "Be Games Ready" and "Trade 2018" programs.
  2. A published, timed tender pipeline for event-linked contracts, so smaller local suppliers can plan and bid early — rather than the pipeline being visible only to the national contractors already in the room.
  3. Deliberately extend the spending window outward — active promotion directing race-weekend visitors toward neighbouring precincts (South Melbourne, St Kilda Road, the Albert Park village strip), not just concessions inside the circuit fence, which is exactly the kind of "business leveraging" the sport-event literature distinguishes from passive impact.
2
Government & AGPC

Make the Ledger Public

  1. Apply Local Jobs First–style local-content minimums to AGPC's own event contracts, the same standard Victoria already sets for other government-funded major projects.
  2. Publish an annual local-leverage report alongside the existing financial one — SME participation rate, local subcontractor spend, and local apprenticeships, in the same table format Gold Coast 2018's case study literature used to show real business benefit rather than "legacy rhetoric."
  3. Adopt event leverage as formal policy, not just an event-week talking point — commission the independent cost-benefit review this site already recommends, and separately, regardless of that review's verdict, formalise a leverage strategy so future hosting years capture more local benefit even if the topline cost-benefit balance never turns positive.
3
The Public

Where the Benefit Lands

  1. A public scorecard that names where money actually goes — not a single headline "$300M" claim, but a line-by-line account of which programs, precincts, or funds any confirmed net benefit is directed to.
  2. A defined share of any confirmed net benefit directed to community programs — directly answering existing, cited criticism that the Grand Prix's net cost could instead double funding for community sport, by making reinvestment a real mechanism rather than a rhetorical trade-off.
  3. Independent, standing oversight tied to the same Auditor-General-scoped review recommended elsewhere on this site, so "benefit" claims are checked to the same standard the cost side already is.
Precedent · 2017–2018

The Playbook Already Exists — Just Not Here

Gold Coast 2018 Commonwealth Games, Queensland

Before a single Commonwealth Games event was run, the Queensland Government had already launched a dedicated small-business leverage push: "Be Games Ready," "Be My Guest," "Mentoring for Growth," and a "Trade 2018" export-readiness stream, backed by dedicated small-business grant programs. The government's own framing was explicit about the difference between hosting and leveraging: the Games would be "a game changer for Queensland business, with legacy outcomes set to flow on long after the Games" — not just four days of visitor spend at the venue gates.

Independent academic research published after the fact backs the substance of that claim rather than just the press release: a peer-reviewed case study in the journal Event Management found measurable business benefits flowing from the leverage programs specifically, distinct from the passive economic impact of the Games themselves — precisely the impact/leverage distinction this page is built on.

No comparably documented program has been identified for the Australian Grand Prix in Melbourne. That is the gap this page argues should close — not because the Gold Coast model would transfer perfectly, but because it proves a genuine SME-leverage program for a major event is a solved problem elsewhere in Australia, not a hypothetical one.

What's In It for Everyone

The common version of this debate treats "worth it" as a single number. This is what it looks like broken out by who actually benefits, if the steps above were adopted.

Local businesses & SMEs

Earlier visibility into event-linked contracts, real support to bid for them, and a deliberate push to route race-weekend spending into surrounding precincts instead of just the circuit gate.

Government & Treasury

A defensible, published leverage record turns "we host Formula 1" from a recurring budget-estimates liability into a program with visible, checkable local return — independent of how the cost-benefit number itself lands.

AGPC, the corporation

A documented local-leverage program is a genuine, citable answer the next time its economic claims are challenged — replacing a disputed headline number with a checkable local-benefit record.

The general public

Visibility into where any confirmed benefit actually lands, and a real mechanism — not just a rhetorical trade-off — for redirecting some of it toward the community programs the event's cost is currently weighed against.

What this page is, and isn't

This is an original proposal, built by combining a peer-reviewed academic model of event leverage with a real, documented precedent from another Australian major event and Victoria's own existing procurement policy. It has not been announced, endorsed, or committed to by the Victorian Government, AGPC, or Formula 1 — and it isn't a substitute for the independent cost-benefit review recommended elsewhere on this site. It's this framework's answer to the more useful question the "is it worth it" debate usually skips: worth it for whom, and what would it actually take to make it so.

The One-Page Version

Executive Summary

The Australian Grand Prix: Closing a 19-Year Independent Review Gap

Prepared by: Ari · August 2026

The Issue

Victoria has committed to hosting the Formula 1 Australian Grand Prix through 2037, at a most-recently-audited net cost of $102.3M in a single year (FY2024-25), on top of a co-hosted MotoGP loss of $26.6M. Cumulative losses already exceed $1 billion and are projected to reach roughly $3 billion by contract end. No independent economic review has tested the benefit side of this commitment since a 2006 Auditor-General-commissioned study found a net social loss.

What This Framework Adds

A three-part measurement standard — direct activity, an honest multiplier range, and a separately reported soft-power index — applied transparently to the 2026 event using only publicly available, cited data. It does not take a side in the "keep it or cut it" debate; it makes the next debate possible to have with real numbers.

~$324M
Government/industry claim, scaled to 2026 attendance
~$107M
Framework's bottom-up direct visitor spend estimate
−$102.3M
Audited net cash cost, FY2024-25 (undisputed)

Why the Gap Exists

Claimed figures use gross-spending/GSP models with a multiplier the ABS discontinued as unsuitable in 2001–02; independent reviews use welfare economics and have twice found net losses (2006, 2013). Neither side currently publishes a methodology the other accepts.

Recommendation

Commission an independent, Auditor-General-scoped cost-benefit review before the next Victorian Budget cycle — the same model used in 2006 — and publish the AGPC's visitor-mix microdata alongside the annual report so third parties can replicate, not just cite, the headline number.

Confidence & Caveats

Direct-spend and net-cost figures are drawn from AGPC's own audited annual report and Victorian Parliament Hansard. The 2026-specific direct-spend estimate is a bottom-up calculation built from 2023/24 visitor-mix ratios (transparently stated) applied to 2026 attendance, not an independently audited number — that is precisely the gap this paper argues should be closed.

Why This Project Matters.

Victoria's Formula 1 contract runs to 2037 and will shape how the state talks about major-event value for over a decade — but the numbers behind it are written by whoever commissions them, and rarely checked by anyone else. This is a small, serious attempt to make that system more navigable for the people it actually affects.

For the Public & Stakeholders

For Victorian taxpayers, a single trustworthy number beats four contradictory ones — it's the difference between an informed opinion and an assumption made on your behalf. For government and the Australian Grand Prix Corporation, a framework that survives scrutiny is what protects a $3 billion, fifteen-year public commitment from becoming a political liability at the next budget estimates hearing. For investors and the wider major-events industry, credible, repeatable measurement is what rebuilds confidence in economic-impact claims generally, after years of multiplier disputes eroding trust in the number itself. And for journalists, policy staff, and anyone deciding whether to believe the next $300 million headline, this is a way to check it — instead of just picking a side based on who said it.

Why This Matters to Me

This project sits at the intersection of motorsport, public policy and economics. It started with a documented, verifiable gap in how the Grand Prix's value gets measured, and grew into an attempt to close it — reading the primary sources directly, working through how direct spending, multiplier modelling and soft power actually relate to one another, and building something that reflects that reading rather than a general impression of it. It is a genuinely self-directed piece of work. If it helps one person ask a sharper question the next time a headline number gets quoted, it has done what it set out to do.
