The Index Lag Protocol

Closing the structural lag between a geopolitical shock and a passive fund's ability to respond

CASE STUDY — RUSSIA, FEB–MAR 2022
Prepared by Ari, ANU
Updated 7 SEP 2026
The problem, precisely stated

When Russia invaded Ukraine on 24 February 2022, BlackRock's funds carried roughly $17bn of Russia exposure that lost value as sanctions and trading restrictions made those securities effectively unsellable — and BlackRock, Vanguard, and every other passive manager tracking MSCI or FTSE Russell emerging-market benchmarks were structurally bound to keep holding those securities until the index providers themselves acted.1

That gap — between a shock the world can see and an index reconstitution a passive mandate requires before a manager can act — is not a Russia-specific problem. It is a standing feature of index-tracking mandates, and it will recur the next time a G7-sanctioned economy sits inside a major benchmark. This protocol proposes what a passive manager can pre-agree today, before the next event, rather than improvise during it.

The takeaway is not that index providers were slow by some absolute standard — MSCI and FTSE Russell both moved inside two weeks, which is fast by index-governance norms. It is that a passive mandate has no lawful mechanism to front-run that window, however obvious the shock. This protocol is built to manage that exact-sized gap — not by front-running the index, but by using every degree of freedom a fund actually retains during it.

Positioning

Nothing above asks an index provider to move faster than its own governance allows, and nothing asks a passive manager to stop being passive. It pre-agrees, in writing and in advance, the parts of the response that are already legal and already available — so that the next Russia-scale event costs a fund the eleven-to-thirteen days index providers actually need, not the additional weeks lost to first figuring out who to call.
