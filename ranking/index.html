Why This Matters

What this tool is, who it's for, and where the numbers come from

What the problem actually is, who has a stake in it, what each of the seven criteria is really asking, and exactly where every number came from.

01 — Who this affects, and why

Australia's transit agencies buy driverless trains and signalling systems roughly once a generation, and those systems then run for 30+ years inside the country's critical infrastructure — carrying millions of passengers a year. When agencies compare bids, price is easy to compare: it's just a number. Things like who's in control of the software running the trains, or who can get into it remotely after it's installed, are much harder to put a number on — so there's rarely a clear, public rule for weighing one against the other. In practice, that usually means the cheapest bid wins by default, not because anyone stopped caring about security, but because nothing requires agencies to weigh the two openly before signing. This scorecard is one attempt at a rule for doing that, built entirely from information that's already public.

Board & Executives

A credible, neutral scorecard is a market-shaping asset for any rail manufacturer — it turns "security-conscious procurement" from a slogan into something a bid can actually be measured against and win on, instead of losing to price alone.

Bid & Government-Affairs Teams

There's currently no consistent rubric that makes a supplier's security credentials legible to a procurement panel weighing them against a cheaper competitor. This gives that argument a structure and a citation trail, not just a claim in a cover letter.

Transit Agencies & Government

Agencies are making a decades-long infrastructure decision under real political pressure, usually with no standard way to weigh "this bid is $50M cheaper" against "this bid raises supply-chain concerns." A published, criteria-based framework replaces ad hoc, politically-loaded judgement calls with something repeatable and defensible.

General Public & Taxpayers

These are public-money decisions about systems that carry millions of commuters every year. When there's no consistent way to weigh security against cost, the public usually only learns why a decision was made well after the contract is already signed — if at all.

02 — What each criterion actually measures

How to read the scores

Every bidder is scored 0–5 on each of the seven criteria below, using only information that's publicly available — no confidential bids, no classified assessments. A criterion's weight is how much it counts toward the final 0–100 total; the heaviest-weighted criterion is the one judged most likely to actually cause a security failure if it goes wrong, not the one that sounds the most alarming. Where no public evidence exists either way, that's marked unverified and scored cautiously rather than assumed to be fine — you can see exactly which scores that affects in the heatmap's dashed borders.

03 — Where this information comes from

Sourcing rules

Every score in this tool traces to a public source — see the tab for the full, numbered list. Company self-disclosures (certifications, ownership pages, press releases) are marked Primary; official government or court records (DOJ settlements, government procurement policy) are marked Government; independent news or investigative reporting is marked Secondary. Nothing here is a classified or clearance-grade assessment — some genuinely relevant facts (a specific contract's remote-access terms, a company's full tier-2 supplier list) simply aren't public for any bidder, and are scored as unverified rather than guessed at.

The problem

Transit agencies have no consistent, defensible way to weigh security and foreign-interference risk against price in rail procurement — so price wins by default. This isn't hypothetical: New South Wales's (NSW) own procurement policy requires agencies to disclose the relative importance of tender evaluation criteria, and mandates a minimum 10% non-price weighting for small and medium enterprise (SME) participation on contracts over $3M — but sets no equivalent mandated minimum for security or national-interest criteria. The gap is procedural, not just political.

Who's bidding

Siemens Mobility

The rail division of Siemens AG, a publicly listed German industrial group. No state ownership at any level of its corporate structure — one of the two largest rail-equipment manufacturers in this field.

Alstom

French rolling-stock and signalling manufacturer, listed on Euronext Paris. No state entity currently holds a controlling stake — its largest shareholder is Canada's CDPQ pension fund — though the French state guaranteed a 2004 rescue loan.

Hyundai Rotem / UGL

A joint bid pairing Hyundai Rotem (the rail-manufacturing arm of South Korea's Hyundai Motor Group) with UGL, an Australian rail-maintenance and engineering firm owned by CIMIC Group, itself majority-controlled by Spain's ACS/Hochtief.

MTR–CRRC Consortium

A joint bid pairing MTR Corporation (Hong Kong's metro operator, roughly 75% owned by the Hong Kong SAR Government) with CRRC, China's state-owned rolling-stock manufacturer — the world's largest by output — overseen by China's State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission (SASAC).

Why this reads as judgment, not advocacy

A scorecard that never costs any bidder anything isn't neutral, it's marketing. Applied symmetrically, compliance/integrity history costs multiple bidders real points: Alstom's 2014 bribery settlement ($772M, the largest Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) fine at the time) and Siemens AG's 2008 settlement ($800M, the record before Alstom's) both score below a clean record — and MTR's Hung Hom scandal and CRRC's South Africa "Locorruption" links do the same on the other side. Ownership transparency, in turn, doesn't penalise state links as such — it penalises the absence of disclosed governance safeguards around them.

Using this in a bid response

Ask how it's weighted, in writing

Before drafting a word of your response, find out how heavily the tender actually weighs security against price. If the Request for Proposal (RFP — the document that spells out what's being bought and how bids will be judged) doesn't say, ask the agency directly: NSW policy already requires agencies to disclose the relative importance of each evaluation criterion for procurements it covers, so this is a fair, and usually answerable, question.

Show it, don't just say it

A certification, an audit report, or a specific contract clause about who can access your systems counts for more with an evaluator than a paragraph claiming your company "takes security seriously." This scorecard's seven criteria are all things that can be pointed to in writing — treat that as a checklist for what your bid response should actually include.

Don't hide a compliance history — explain it

If your company has a past bribery or integrity finding, assume the evaluator already knows or will find out. A finding that's been fully resolved — with a completed independent monitorship and real time elapsed since — reads very differently from an unresolved one, but only if you're the one who explains that history, rather than leaving it to be discovered mid-evaluation.

Close the gaps that are just undisclosed, not actually bad

Several of this framework's lower scores exist simply because information isn't public yet — a tier-2 supplier list, a specific remote-access policy — not because the underlying answer is bad. Publishing what your company already does internally often costs nothing, and can move your own score up under this framework or one like it.
