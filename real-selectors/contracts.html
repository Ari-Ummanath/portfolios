Case file 02 · live & unresolved

Sell the team.
Keep the contract.

Cricket Australia (CA) has voted to open the Big Bash League (BBL) to private — including Indian — capital, while insisting it keeps the actual levers: scheduling, salary caps, player availability. What happens when a board tries to sell ownership without selling control.

Two ways to run a contract list

The privatisation log

The India-Australia pathway here isn't players moving between systems — it's capital. Indian money is circling Australian domestic cricket just as Cricket Australia tries to keep contract control out of investors' hands.

8–10 Sep 2026

Cricket Australia approves opening BBL/WBBL ownership to private equity. Melbourne Renegades go to market first for a full sale ahead of 2027–28; other franchises may sell up to a 49% stake. CA states it retains international scheduling, salary caps, player availability, branding and investor approval. ReadCricket ↗ · Indian Link ↗

10 Sep 2026

IPL-linked capital circles. Reporting says Indian investors, including owners associated with Indian Premier League (IPL) franchises, “have shown interest” — no confirmed deals or named buyers yet. IPL owners are described as typically preferring outright ownership or real operational control, which sits awkwardly against CA's 49% ceiling — a caution echoed by a comparison to English cricket's more investor-friendly ownership model, and by the gap in Pat Cummins's own playing time: 7 BBL appearances against 76 IPL appearances since 2016. Business Standard ↗ · Yahoo Sports ↗

Ongoing, Sep 2026

The ACA rejects the current proposal. Its dispute: whether privatisation proceeds count toward “Australian Cricket Revenue,” of which players currently receive 27.5% — the ACA wants 33%, a formal player agreement first, and an independent arbiter. It states no team sale can proceed without its sign-off; CA CEO Todd Greenberg says the aim is to “put more money into the hands of the players.” Ministry of Sport ↗

Context

Not every state is on board. New South Wales called the privatisation push a “sugar hit” even as its board approved the plan on the night of 7 Sep 2026; Tasmania, Victoria and Western Australia supported it, while Queensland's association has also resisted. The current player MOU runs through 2028. ReadCricket ↗ · ABC News ↗

Exhibit C · original argument

Selling equity, keeping control

Cricket Australia's whole strategy is a bet that you can sell the team without selling control of the cricketer.

What's being sold

  • Franchise equity — up to 100% of Melbourne Renegades, up to 49% elsewhere
  • A share of roughly $1bn+ CA projects the process could generate
  • Access, for foreign (including Indian) capital, to a growing global T20 asset class

What CA insists it keeps

  • International scheduling and the primacy of Test cricket
  • Salary caps and player availability — the actual contract levers
  • Investor approval and licence reserve prices
How the two cases connect

Taken together, the two files here make the same point from opposite directions. Cricket Canada shows what happens with no separation between who controls a federation and who it lets play: a board itself allegedly captured, deciding selection. Cricket Australia is trying to build that separation deliberately — sell the equity, keep the calendar and the salary cap — while its players' association pushes back, arguing for a larger share of the money and a say in how the deal is structured. Neither case is finished. Both are about the same asset: a contracted cricketer, and who actually gets to decide what happens to them.

Exhibit D

Who's entangled, and what each needs

Six stakeholders here too, weighing the same underlying question from a different angle.

Governance

Cricket Australia board & CEO Todd Greenberg

At stake: $1bn+ projected, and control of the sport's commercial future

An ACA agreement — the sale legally can't proceed without it.

Players

Australian Cricketers' Association

At stake: revenue share (27.5% vs. its 33% ask) and a say in what counts as “Australian Cricket Revenue”

A formal agreement and an independent arbiter before any proceeds move.

States

State associations

At stake: uneven exposure — Victoria moves first, NSW and Queensland resist

Clarity on whether opting out stays viable once other states have sold.

Capital

IPL-linked investors (individual franchise owners, not the BCCI)

At stake: access to a global T20 franchise market, where BBL playing time already trails the IPL — Pat Cummins has made 7 BBL appearances against 76 in the IPL since 2016

Real operational control, which CA's 49% ceiling (outside the Renegades sale) limits; commentary compares CA unfavourably to English cricket's more investor-friendly model on this point. The BCCI itself has made no formal statement on BBL privatisation — this is individual private capital, not institutional Indian cricket.

Public

Australian fans & the domestic game

At stake: whether the Big Bash stays recognisably Australian, and Test cricket stays primary

Transparent reporting on what “primacy of Test cricket” means once outside capital has a seat at the table.
