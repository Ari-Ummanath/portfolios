The SAF Mandate Gap

A sequencing framework for domestic sustainable aviation fuel: investment certainty, feedstock integrity, and public-value safeguards.

LAST REVIEWED 6 SEP 2026

The situation, in one paragraph

Australia has started funding sustainable aviation fuel.

The question still unresolved is how to create dependable demand for it. A domestic SAF industry needs more than grants — it needs a credible pathway from project funding to long-term offtake, one that protects travellers, regions, communities, and taxpayers along the way. This is a living policy project: it maps the gap, tests the constraints, and sets out a draftable route forward.

The problem, plainly

There is no guaranteed buyer for domestic SAF

Qantas and Airbus asked the government for two things in 2023: capital support to build production, and a legislated blending mandate to guarantee demand once that fuel existed. Government has delivered the first. It has not yet delivered the second — and without a mandate, no investor will fund a production facility on the strength of grants alone.

The purpose of this project

Turn "should we have a mandate" into "how would it work"

This project takes that unresolved question off the shelf and designs an answer: a staged timeline, a named compliance entity, a cost-containment mechanism, and a stakeholder sequence — so that whichever department eventually drafts the mandate has less work left to do.

Why I built this

I built it because Qantas and Airbus put an unusually specific, well-evidenced policy request on the public record in 2023, government spent two years responding to half of it, and almost nobody went back to check what happened to the other half. That gap — between what gets announced and what gets legislated — is where most of the interesting policy work in this country actually sits.

The sequencing question

Legislate the mandate architecture before 2030 — and comply conditionally, not on a fixed date.

A blending target, capital grants, and a buy-out safeguard cannot be designed independently. A target without supply support raises compliance costs; grants without durable demand leave revenue uncertain; a mandate without a buy-out can expose airlines, fuel suppliers, and travellers to a shortage-driven price spike. The Government has committed funding toward a regulatory impact analysis of mandates and other demand-side measures, and confirmed in the 2026–27 Budget that it will introduce a demand measure — the design questions below are about how.1

The mandate should turn on only once both keys are ready

Key one

Investment certainty

Cleaner Fuels Program guidelines issued; first projects show a credible pathway to final investment decision; certification protocol functioning.

Key two

Public-interest protection

Buy-out mechanism set; regional-service safeguards in place; competition and pass-through reporting designed; supply-shortage protocol published.

A mandate should be legislated early, signalled clearly, and activated responsibly.

Production support

Two distinct programs, easily conflated

The A$250m Future Made in Australia Innovation Fund (2026–29) reduces technical, feedstock, and first-of-a-kind development risk at the pre-commercial and demonstration stage.3 The A$1.1bn Cleaner Fuels Program (2027 onward) addresses the operating-cost and revenue gap once a project begins producing eligible fuel.4 Neither is a durable demand obligation; aligning their eligibility and lifecycle thresholds with the eventual mandate is itself a design task.

Demand pathway

Obligate suppliers, not airlines

Placing the compliance obligation on fuel suppliers — not individual airlines — avoids disadvantaging one carrier over another and creates a market-wide mechanism rather than an airline-by-airline tax. Airlines still shape the market through offtake agreements, book-and-claim purchases, and joint investment. The obligation should carry a defined annual volume, a lifecycle-emissions eligibility rule, tradeable certificates, limited banking, a shortage protocol, and a review every three to five years.

Cost containment

A safety valve, not a routine substitute for SAF

ICF's modelling proposed an illustrative buy-out price of A$4–5 per litre as a consumer-protection mechanism, alongside a package under which the modelled cost impact stayed close to neutral out to 2040.2 This project treats that as a 2023 modelling input, not a recommended 2026 figure or settled policy. A durable design should instead set the buy-out through an independently published, annually reviewed methodology — indexed to a transparent SAF/jet-fuel price differential, with a floor and a ceiling, and revenue ring-fenced for SAF-enabling infrastructure, certification, and regional airport logistics. This is the direct policy answer to "producers need offtake certainty before they will commit capital" — the public record is clear that Government has committed to test demand-side design options, including a mandate, a low-carbon fuel standard, and certificate-based mechanisms, precisely because that certainty does not yet exist.1

Addressed to federal policymakers

Decision requested

Publish and consult on a legislated, aviation-specific SAF mandate framework by 2027, with compliance commencing from 2030 only after predefined supply, certification, and public-interest readiness conditions are met.

Recommended architecture

Legislate a supplier-level pathway; make compliance conditional rather than uncertain; pair it with the existing grant instruments; retain a SAF-specific sub-target so aviation is not crowded out by lower-cost renewable-diesel pathways; build cost containment into the statute; and treat feedstock governance — traceability, land-use safeguards, community and First Nations engagement — as core infrastructure, not an afterthought.

Immediate next step

Commission an exposure draft linking the mandate pathway, Cleaner Fuels Program eligibility, Guarantee of Origin development, and regional-connectivity safeguards — so consultation can move from whether a demand measure is needed to what design can deliver one responsibly.

Blocker analysis

Three implementation questions, not one failure of will

The evidence points to three connected questions that have to be resolved together, not one simple explanation. The first is timing: when can an obligation begin without outrunning certified supply? The second is allocation: how can policy reserve enough low-carbon fuel for aviation while recognising other sectors' needs? The third is protection: how can government create investment-grade demand without placing disproportionate cost or service risk on passengers and regional communities?

A. Sequence before scale

Government's low-carbon-liquid-fuels consultation says explicitly that it is assessing the interaction between supply-side incentives and demand-side options, including mandates and a low-carbon fuel standard, through a regulatory impact analysis.1 The Cleaner Fuels Program is designed for projects nearing a final investment decision, but its guidelines and eligibility criteria are still being developed with input from project developers, feedstock suppliers, and financiers.2 The next move available to government is not "announce a mandate tomorrow" — it is to publish a Mandate Readiness Framework alongside the Cleaner Fuels Program guidelines: confirm fuel suppliers as the compliance entity, settle lifecycle-emissions and certification rules, announce the mandate pathway ahead of final investment decisions, and start compliance only once certified supply and certificate infrastructure exist.

B. Feedstock has competing destinations

SAF, renewable diesel, and naphtha can be produced in shared facilities from overlapping feedstocks. Because renewable diesel is nominally cheaper to produce, a technology-neutral policy risks channelling scarce feedstock and capital toward it — leaving aviation, which has fewer near-term substitutes, short of fuel. Qantas and Airbus's own joint submission makes this argument directly, and recommends at minimum a SAF sub-mandate with tradeable certificates as the fix.3 Government's own consultation paper recognises the same tension: low-carbon liquid fuels will serve aviation, heavy road freight, maritime, mining, agriculture, and construction, and feedstock cost — driven partly by competing demand for agricultural land and food crops — is already the primary driver of production cost.1

This is not only a climate question. A generic low-carbon-fuel standard is a product-allocation instrument as much as an emissions one — it decides which sector gets first claim on a scarce input. That framing sits inside a broader pattern of contested demand across Australia's critical resource inputs, a pattern this author has examined elsewhere in co-authored research on critical-resource-supply strategy for the ANU Strategic and Defence Studies Centre.4

C. Price protection is policy infrastructure

Low-carbon liquid fuels are currently estimated at two to five times the cost of their fossil equivalents, and government's own framing is to deliver public value while minimising price impacts and protecting competition.1 ICF's package addresses this directly through a buy-out price, modelling a broadly cost-neutral effect on fares out to 2040 under the combined mandate-plus-grants scenario — a modelled result, not a guarantee.5 The differentiating design choice this project recommends is a Regional Connectivity Safeguard: a narrowly scoped, time-limited exemption for very small-volume regional uplift where supply is demonstrably unavailable; buy-out revenue ring-fenced for domestic SAF infrastructure and regional blending capacity; annual public reporting on pass-through by route class; a mandatory review if regional fares or frequency move past set thresholds; and a bar on the safeguard being used as a blanket exemption for large incumbent carriers.

Where upstream risk actually sits

A mandate creates demand for a finished fuel. Investment risk sits upstream — in feedstock availability, traceability, technology yield, transport logistics, and contracted offtake. Who has to move, and in what order

Neither an airline nor a manufacturer can clear this alone

Industry support for domestic SAF is substantial, but implementation interests are not identical. The workable sequence is for government to settle certification, fiscal, and mandate architecture first — then align suppliers, producers, airlines, airports, feedstock providers, and communities around the operational rules. How claims are labelled

Three kinds of statement appear throughout this project, and they are not interchangeable. A Record is a fact drawn directly from a primary or statutory source — a program's funding total, an announcement date. An Assessment is this project's reading of what several records together imply — a status chip, a "why this matters" line. A Proposal is a design recommendation made by this project and is not government policy — the sequencing table, the buy-out methodology, the Regional Connectivity Safeguard. ICF's modelled figures are treated as an industry benchmark throughout, never as an enacted target.
