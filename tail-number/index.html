01 · The problem in two minutes

A safety system that doubles as a public locator

Modern aircraft carry a transponder system called ADS-B Out (Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast). Several times a second it broadcasts the aircraft's position, altitude and a permanent identity code. Air traffic control relies on it, and regulators require it on business jets across most controlled airspace in the United States, Europe, Australia and Canada.19181526 The broadcast is not encrypted, so anyone with a receiver can pick it up.20

Thousands of hobbyist receivers now feed these signals to public websites. Match the identity code against a national aircraft register and a flight becomes a named person's or company's movements. Some sites filter out aircraft whose owners have asked for privacy; others state that they never do.11

The United States has built three partial protections: a display-blocking list called LADD (Limiting Aircraft Data Displayed), temporary anonymous identity codes called PIA (Privacy ICAO Address, where ICAO is the International Civil Aviation Organization, the UN's aviation agency), and, since 2025, the option to withhold an owner's name and address from the public register.215 According to the US National Business Aviation Association (NBAA), other countries do not currently offer the anonymous-code option.4 Australia's public register lists the names and addresses of registration holders and operators, and Canada's regulator may publish owners' addresses.1625

iWhat this means

A private jet constantly tells anyone listening where it is, a bit like a phone with location sharing permanently switched on. Governments can control what their own data feeds show and what their aircraft registers publish, but they cannot stop private receivers hearing an unencrypted broadcast. Real privacy therefore has to start at the broadcast itself, by hiding who the aircraft belongs to. Only the United States offers that, and only on domestic flights.

Illustrative case

@ElonJet and the limits of "it's public data"

In December 2022 Twitter suspended @ElonJet, an account run by a 20-year-old Florida student that automatically posted the movements of Elon Musk's jet using publicly available flight data. It had more than 500,000 followers. A month earlier Musk had said he would not ban it. After the suspension, Twitter said real-time location posting breached its doxxing rules, while "delayed posting of locations" was acceptable.9

In early 2024 lawyers for Taylor Swift sent the same student a cease-and-desist letter alleging "stalking and harassing behavior". He maintained that he compiled only public information.10 Both episodes rest on the same fact: the data is lawful to collect, and the risk comes from combining it and publishing it in real time.

Why it matters to the industry

Every jet sold is exposed

Because every business jet flying in controlled airspace broadcasts ADS-B, the gap touches every owner, operator and manufacturer in the sector, wherever the aircraft is registered. For a manufacturer whose customers pay for discretion, the gap is a product and relationship issue as well as a policy one.

For example, Bombardier completed the sale of its rail division in January 2021, which it said "completes Bombardier's repositioning as a pure-play business jet company."23 For a company like that, every aircraft it sells carries the exposure described here.

06 · Recommendations

What a manufacturer could do now, next and later

Three horizons, each with a lead team and a measure of success, written for a business-jet manufacturer and using Bombardier as the worked example. The near-term steps need no change in law. The later steps use a manufacturer's standing to help shape it. The same steps apply to other manufacturers, operators and management companies.

Bombardier is used only as an illustrative example. This case study is independent and is not affiliated with, endorsed by or prepared for Bombardier. The recommendations are the author's proposals; the evidence they rest on is cited. This is general information and guidance, not legal advice.

Now 0–6 MONTHS

Lead · Customer experience

A privacy-at-delivery pack for every new and in-service aircraft.

  • For US-registered aircraft: LADD enrolment, a PIA eligibility check (US-registered, 1090 MHz ADS-B, third-party call sign), and a register withholding request through FAA CARES.215
  • Explain the limits plainly. PIA is US airspace only ("a flight from NYC to London is not allowed"), and unfiltered sites ignore block lists.111
  • For Australian, Canadian, UK and EU customers: set out what is unavailable and suggest they seek their own legal advice on registration structures.

Measure · share of eligible deliveries enrolled

Next 6–18 MONTHS

Lead · Government affairs

Champion an Australian equivalent of the US protections.

  • A submission to the Australian Government, CASA and Airservices proposing three measures: a display-limiting list for data Airservices shares, an anonymous-address option in Australian airspace, and an opt-out for owner details on the public register.16
  • Frame it as security and safety policy rather than secrecy, citing the stalking allegations and M&A inference evidence.107
  • Invite Australian business-jet operators to co-sign the submission, so regulators hear from the owners affected and not only from a manufacturer.
  • Bombardier is headquartered in Canada, where owners' addresses may be published and no anonymous-code scheme exists, so a parallel brief to Transport Canada would fit.23254

Measure · consultation opened; jurisdictions with a program

Later 18 MONTHS +

Lead · Engineering & ICAO engagement

Make privacy a product feature and a global standard.

  • Engineer avionics so an address change is a quick software task. Early PIA users reported having to remove equipment to reprogram it for international flights.14
  • Support an ICAO standard for rotating privacy addresses, building on the A41 and A42 Assembly papers.2120
  • Design against re-identification. Peer-reviewed modelling shows program design changes can cut traceability sharply.8

Stress test

The strongest objections, and responses

"It's public data broadcast by law. Privacy is the wrong frame."

True of the broadcast, and the brief does not propose restricting who may receive it. The US has already accepted that lawful public data can still warrant limits on registry and display, and put the registry opt-out into statute in 2024.6 The proposal copies that settled approach.

"Tracking holds the powerful to account."

Public-interest scrutiny of how private and state aircraft are used is legitimate. Any Australian scheme should keep access for regulators, law enforcement and accredited investigators. It should aim at real-time public exposure of individuals rather than historical accountability. The US PIA design keeps identity available to the FAA through the third-party call sign.1

"Controls don't work anyway, because receivers are everywhere."

Partly right. Industry coverage concedes that completely restricting this information is "nearly impossible".22 That is why the brief puts the broadcast layer (anonymous, rotating addresses) ahead of display lists, which research shows leak.7

"Anonymous addresses could weaken air safety."

PIA runs inside the FAA's own system. Since December 2024 it also supports controller–pilot data link services when the PIA address and registration are filed in the flight plan.13 Safety identification is preserved and only public linkage is removed.

08 · Findings & terms

Key findings, and what the terms mean

The ten observations the analysis rests on, followed by plain definitions of every abbreviation used on this page.

  1. Policy mixes up two problems: display and broadcast.LADD controls what trackers show from FAA feeds. It "does not impact the ADS-B broadcast data".1 Owners can reasonably believe they are "blocked" when they are only filtered.
  2. The register is the key that decodes the broadcast.An identity code only matters once it is matched to an owner. The UK register publishes owners' names and addresses with no opt-out.17 Australia's shows holder and operator addresses.16 The US now lets owners withhold theirs.5
  3. Every US safeguard has a known gap.LADD misses unfiltered sites, PIA stops at the border, and records remain subject to FOIA unless an exemption applies.1113 Layering helps, but no single layer is enough.
  4. Privacy wears off over time.Anonymous codes can be re-linked from flight patterns. Modelling found 69.2% of a simulated fleet still identifiable after 100 days.8 Rotation frequency is therefore a security parameter, and the FAA now allows a new code every 20 days.13
  5. International travel resets protection.International operations require the aircraft's original code.4 For long-range jets, the most discreet trips are often the international ones.
  6. Friction limits uptake.By late 2020 the PIA program had 74 users. Operators reported needing to remove hardware to reprogram it.14 This is where a manufacturer can help.
  7. Canada, home to a major business-jet maker, has the same gaps.ADS-B has been required in Class A airspace since 2023, the regulator may publish owners' addresses, and no anonymous-code scheme exists.26254
  8. Australia has none of the three layers.ADS-B has been mandatory for IFR aircraft since February 2017.15 No display list, anonymous-code scheme or register opt-out was identified. A 2025 public FOI request asking Airservices for any suppression policy had not produced documents when last updated.24
  9. The harms are both corporate and personal.They range from M&A signals to alleged stalking.710 This widens the coalition beyond owners to boards, insurers and security teams.
  10. Momentum is building at ICAO.Assembly papers in 2022 and 2025 called for mechanisms including dynamic addressing and national rules on publishing ADS-B data.2120
